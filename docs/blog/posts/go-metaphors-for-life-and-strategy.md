Ever since being introduced to Go in grad school, I've loved the game. It's simple. I can teach you the rules in 5 minutes and you won't likely forget them. Basically, each player gets black stones or white stones. You place a stone anywhere on the board during your turn. If your stone becomes completely surrounded (e.g. if every space it's connected to contains a stone of another color) then the stone is captured. _But_ if you extend your stone by connecting it to more of your stones, then the whole group must be surrounded by stones of the other color in order to kill it.

That's how to play. That's it. The only other chunk you need to learn is how to score. During the laying down of stones, each player creates bounded territories that they control. At the end of the game, each player gets a point for every open position they control, and a point for every captured stone or stone in their territory that is obviously capturable (called "dead" stones).

From Go's simplicity there emerges a great wealth of complexity and strategy. And "emerge" really is the right word here, much of the complexity is an _emergent property_ of the simple rule set. Case in point – two eyes. If you form a group of stones that has two separate open spaces ("eyes"), then that group can not be captured. This isn't a rule of the game, but is just an outcome of the rules already mentioned above. Why can't you kill a group that has two eyes? It's because you can only put down one stone at a time and killing a shape with two eyes would require you to put down two stones simultaneously – which you can't do.

Something I like even more about Go are the metaphors that seem to apply well in strategy and tactics of everyday life. The following list was me + AI working together to collect a set of those metaphors, and I wanted to put this in my blog here so that I could refer back to it and add to it later. Enjoy!

## 1. Win the Right Battle

Tactical excellence does not compensate for strategic irrelevance. You can fight brilliantly for a corner and still lose the game because the corner never mattered.

Before figuring out how to win, ask whether this is a fight worth winning.

## 2. Tenuki: Play Somewhere Else

In Go, *tenuki* means ignoring your opponent's last move and playing in a different part of the board. Beginners find this almost physically painful. Experienced players do it all the time.

Not every problem deserves an immediate response. A demand on your attention may not be its highest-value use. Sometimes the strongest reply is to let the provocation sit and play the bigger point instead.

## 3. Find the Biggest Point

On any given turn there are dozens of legal moves. The disciplined question is simple: which available action changes the overall position the most?

Prioritization is the same skill off the board. Not "what is urgent" or "what is easy," but "what moves the needle."

## 4. Avoid Overconcentration

In Go you can keep adding stones to a group that is already safe. Each new stone adds less. Eventually you have built a fortress where a fence would have done, while the rest of the board goes begging.

More resources do not always produce proportionally more value. Build sufficient strength, then deploy scarce resources elsewhere.

## 5. Leave Some Aji

*Aji* – literally "taste" – means latent possibility left in a position. A defect that is not quite a defect yet. Good players often leave it alone on purpose, because fixing everything now costs moves, and the unresolved possibility constrains the opponent.

Not every weakness or uncertainty needs to be resolved immediately. Tolerating imperfection can preserve resources and future possibilities.

## 6. Sacrifice the Stones

Every Go player has to learn this one the hard way: stones you have invested in are not automatically stones worth saving. Chasing a lost group across the board ruins your whole position.

Do not defend something merely because you have invested in it. A commitment is valuable insofar as it improves your future position.

## 7. Territory vs. Influence

Some stones secure concrete territory you can count. Others project influence – potential, pressure, options that may become something later. You cannot win with only one kind.

Some investments produce concrete returns; others build reputation, relationships, knowledge, or leverage. A strong position needs both.

## 8. Keep Sente

*Sente* means initiative. A sente move demands an answer, so you get to move again where you want. *Gote* means you are the one answering. String too many gote moves together and you spend the whole game responding to someone else's plan.

Favor actions that create initiative instead of leaving you perpetually responding. Good moves shape what happens next.

## 9. Build Thickness

Thickness is strength that does not convert directly to points but radiates power across the board. Thick groups cannot be attacked, so you can fight freely around them.

Invest in resilience through savings, health, relationships, skills, and reputation. A strong base gives you freedom to take risks.

## 10. Do Not Cash In Thickness Too Soon

The temptation with thickness is to use it immediately to grab something small and countable. Usually a mistake. The leverage is worth more than the first thing it can buy.

Do not squander a durable advantage for its most obvious short-term payoff. Its leverage may be worth more.

## 11. Think Whole Board

A sequence that looks good in one corner can be bad for the game. Every local fight changes the value of fights everywhere else.

A decision that looks good locally can be bad globally. Judge it by its effect on the larger system.

## 12. Choose Your Direction of Play

Every stone points somewhere. A wall can lean left or lean right, and that choice decides which side of the board becomes yours. The stones are the same; the future they build is not.

Evaluate an action by more than what it accomplishes now. Ask which future possibilities it opens.

## 13. Create Miai

*Miai* means two points that accomplish the same thing, so that if your opponent takes one, you take the other. It is the art of being fine either way.

Build situations with multiple good outcomes. If one path closes, another remains available.

## 14. Position, Do Not Predict

You cannot read out the whole game. Nobody can. Strong players do not win by predicting perfectly; they win by staying strong across many possible continuations.

Instead of planning perfectly for one imagined future, build a position that remains strong across several possibilities.

## 15. Make Multipurpose Moves

The most beautiful moves in Go do several things at once: attack and defend, take territory while building influence, answer a threat while creating a new one. One stone, three jobs.

Look for actions that solve several problems at once, strengthen your position, reduce risk, and open new opportunities. When you find one, play it.

## 16. Use the Natural Terrain

An old proverb says gold corners, silver sides, grass center: the same number of stones enclose the most territory in a corner and the least in the middle. The edge of the board is a wall you do not have to build – two free walls in the corner, one on the side, none in the center.

Build where existing structures do part of the work for you: distribution, trust, habits, institutions, allies already bought in. Do not build in the open field when there is a corner available.
