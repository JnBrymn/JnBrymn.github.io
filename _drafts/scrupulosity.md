Dear fellow spiritual traveler,

You made a wrong turn along the way. Maybe the map was upside down. You find yourself alone in a barren wilderness... that is, aside from the hidden pits that drop down to the depths of hell; aside from tangling, tearing vines that impede your progress; aside from the winds that howl of your loneliness; aside from the silence – the absence of the only voice you _really_ want to hear. There is a bright point along the horizon thought, and you must reach it. ... Or was it a mirage? Why, oh why, couldn't you have been one of those crystal rubbing hippies? At least they act like they've figured it out? At least they seem... at peace?

Scrupulocity is a form of OCD. It preoccupies the sufferer with feelings of excessive religious or moral guilt. In response, the sufferer will seek solace in compulsive rituals. These can be anything – prayer for imagined sins, obsessive study of religious texts, exaggerated adherence to religious rituals. The goal is to just feel "right" again – righteous again – whole again. But the compulsions are a weak balm. Soon, the same old doubts creep back in – "Do my thoughts mean that I'm evil?", "Am I _really_ good enough to get to Heaven?", "Did I commit the unforgivable sin? Maybe even I didn't notice it!" So, to protect yourself, you _avoid_. Maybe you avoid religious stimulus of any kind. Maybe you avoid other religions. Maybe you avoid things that could cause you to "sin" (air-quotes there). Maybe you avoid life all together.

## My Experience

For me, there were some signs early on pointing toward scrupulocity. For instance, prayers must start with "Dear God" and must end with "in Christ name I pray, amen". This was the proper envelope. If either the opening or the closing was missing, then the prayer was invalid – God wouldn't hear it, or maybe He would, but He just wouldn't care. It didn't count. _And_ you had to say a prayer before you went to sleep at night. Much of my life I took a long time to find sound sleep because I would keep dozing off before I had properly ended the prayer. I would rouse myself to say a few more words and work closer to the end – if I didn't say "in Christ name I pray, amen," then it wasn't a real prayer and I hadn't done my duty.

At 18 I headed to college and scrupulocity hit me in full force. A philosophy class my freshman year was probably the tipping point. The professor had gone to seminary to become a Baptist preacher and after graduating had promptly become and athiest. He asked directly all of the questions that had been lying dormant in the back of my mind. _Is the God of the Old Testament really the same as the God of the New Testament? Do we really have a true Will? Or are our actions programmed in? Does God punish people, knowing that they can't help the choices they make?_ He introduced me to new religions. And I was forced to concede that people of all religions were just as good as I thought I was, and just as sincere about their faith.

I never rebelled. Heck, it might have been better if I had. Instead, I leaned upon my religious upbringing and upon rigid logic. My inclination was to _prove_ my way out of my religious conundrums. And there were sooo many conundrums. Things that you might consider valid to think about – the nature of the unforgivable sin; whether to interpret the whole Bible as literal, or if not, then how to know which parts aren't literal; how to be confident that the "important parts" of the Bible were literally true. But I also had lots of concerns that would be alien to most. I've spent months worried about the religious implications of the Many Worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics. On two separate occasions I dropped all of my school work for a span of days to attempt to mathematically parse the famous liar's paradox "This sentence is false". It mattered.

Actually, that's one of the hallmarks of scrupulosity. The concern you have in mind right now _really_ matters. And not resolving the concern will lead to terrible distress. You may be forever damned, God may disappear, life may become nihilistic meaninglessness. So, now we go to the compulsions. Unlike other forms of OCD, the compulsions of scrupulocity are largely mental. This makes scrupulocity particulary difficuly to treat with behavioral therapy because people can see a hand washing compulsion and can help you avoid it, people can't help you avoid your own mind and neither can you.

Things got worse for me. The conundrums would cycle. I would spend a month on some existential or theological concern, but before I could ever solve it fully, another one more terrible would arise and demand my attention – all of it. My school work suffered, I lost weight, everything in life would start to somehow be related to the concern du jour. In my most desperate times, people would notice because, distracted by my thoughts, I could not follow conversations. I withdrew from others so that I could focus and maybe solve the problem.

Depression was never mixed with the anxiety, so I was lucky in that respect, but the anxiety was extreme. I remember asking myself at one point if I would give me left arm just to be forever done with the anxiety I was experiencing – the answer came back, a confident and very literal "yes".

## The Gradual Road to Recovery

Traveler friend, I hate that you have found yourself in your own wilderness. As for me, I did finally make it out. Perhaps you will too. For me, it took a great deal of time. I would estimate that 8 years of my life (my 18th to 26th years) were spent in the wilderness with nothing on the horizon, and over the course of about 5 years I emerged. It's strange though, I didn't "walk out". Walking was efforting on my own, and every step _I chose to take_ in the wilderness was the wrong one. Somehow, giving up on getting out of the wilderness was the key. I would look up occasionally and realize that the terrain was getting better, and I didn't know when it happened.

There were little key moments though that held clues for how I was to escape.

Fairly early on (a few years in) when I was confident that I was absolutely insane, I remember a moment one night at the peak of my anxiety where I just gave up on ever getting better and declared my complacency with being crazy. Counterintuitively, for several days after that, my anxiety was much decreased.

Another thing I noticed is that discussion with my father were strangely _unhelpful_, though I will forever be thankful that my dad loved me so much and was patient enough to try. He would call me regularly, and we would work together, sometimes for hours, to address my conundrums. We would work toward logical ways of addressing my concerns, either by directly trying to solve them, or more often just making it clear that the conundrum was silly to even be worrying about. Inevitably, I would feel better while I spoke with him. But after the call, the insights that I had gained would fade away. I would cling to them, review them, contemplate them, but it wouldn't bring back the confidence I had glimpsed while on the phone. Instead, the thoughts conundrums became stronger.

Getting married was a final big change. With a spouse, I could no longer selfishly sit in a chair and stare at the ceiling for hours at a time. I wasn't for myself. I was for me and my wife. This change was the best behavioral therapy that one could hope for.

## Tips and Tricks for Use in the Wilderness

In the remainder of this post I'll also talk about some tips and tricks that I discovered during my wanderings. I think they helped me. Maybe they'll help you too! But I give you this one warning that will seem paradoxical – these things can only help you if you don't _long_ for them to help you. Most of these tips are about letting go and leaning upon trust rather than your own efforts. _Longing_ is looking at the horizon and begging it to come to you. _Longing_ is mentally practicing your ensnarement.


### Cognitive Behavioral Therapy

CBT. Look it up, read a few books, find a therapist, practice it. The basic idea is a two pronged approach that tackles incorrect cognition and maladaptive behavior. The easy part is cognition. With the help of a therapist you come to understand the irrationality of your thoughts. Ideally you come away from this step saying "yeah, the behaviors I'm engaging in are inconsistent with reason – I should stop them."

The hard part is the behavior. You learn a lot about your own psychology at this point. The goal is to modify the behavior - quit the compulsions. But what you find out is that it's _hard_. The compulsions were your defense against the crippling anxiety. Of course this isn't really what the behaviors do – in the long run the behaviors reinforce the anxiety (and also make you kinda weird). One of the typical tools here is Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) where you trigger the anxiety but resist jumping into the typical compulsion behavior. This can be useful for scrupulosity, but again it's kinda challenging when the compulsion is completely mental. I have some tips below.

The remainder of the tips and tricks things that helped my scrupulosity. Most fall under the CBT umbrella.

### Your Thoughts Aren't You

"Holy crap?! I can't believe that you would be capable of thinking that! What an awful person you must be!"

If you've had this conversation with yourself before, then you esteem your control of your own brain much too highly. You have _some_ control over your thoughts, but don't hold yourself accountable for every thing that flies through your brain. Your brain is not you, it's a computation machine that you are responsible for directing _towards_ the right thoughts. You will get random noise in the process. When naked imagery of your coworkers flies through your head (if that's your thing) then just laugh and let it go. If it's a blasphemy, then just let it fly through. _Definitely_ don't try to "fix" it with a prayer or with a cleansing thought. In doing so, you just draw your brain's attention to that line of thinking, and set yourself up for having more of those thoughts.

### Acknowledge Your Caricature of God

What God do you serve? If the true God is the god of your scrupulosity, we're all cooked! Because _your_ god is just _looking_ for a way to condemn you. _Probably_, you have committed the unforgivable sin. _Probably_ he's holding those recurring inappropriate thoughts against you. _Probably_ you forgot to ask forgiveness for some sin in the past (or maybe you just didn't end it with "in Christ name I pray, amen" ... and the email never was sent!).

I can not imagine that a being powerful enough to create the entire cosmos would look down on his creation just looking for a way to smite us. God isn't the kid on the playground with the magnifying glass spitefully burning ants. No, we are told "Indeed, the very hairs of your head are all numbered. Don’t be afraid; you are worth more than many sparrows." (Luke 12:7).

Again, the God of the Bible says "Ask and it will be given to you; seek and you will find; knock and the door will be opened to you. 8 For everyone who asks receives; the one who seeks finds; and to the one who knocks, the door will be opened." (Matthew 7:7-8) But if you take your scrupulosity to its logical conclusion then the god you serve is likely to greet you at heaven's gates and say "Hello, good and faithful servant – I saw how earnestly you sought me in life... but you didn't quite seek me in the right way. Sorry, you'll need to go down the hall to the next room." The god of your scrupulosity is a caricature.

### Cut All Escape Routes

I mentioned two clues earlier. The relief in the night I completely handed myself over to "insanity" as I put it that night, vs. the many anxious nights that I clung to the whispered wish that I could just get back to normal. The only way out is to let go.

**No wishing allowed** – "Hope is a good thing, maybe the best of things, and no good thing ever dies." ~ Andy from the Shawshank Redemption. But wishes aren't hope. Wishes are a foregone conclusion that the thing you want is no longer reachable, and the only thing that could give you what you seek is a magic grant in answer to your wish. Wishes aren't aimed at God, they're aimed at shooting stars or fairies. Wishes are whispered into the wind. Every time you hear yourself making a wish, shut it down mid-phrase. It is a clinging to something you've already written off as impossible. In particular, never wish that you'll "get better". You will or you won't, but wishes are the links in the chains that bind you.

**Cut your confidantes** – It feels so relieving to have someone to talk to about your strange concerns, but ween yourself off. I've attended therapy briefly on several occasions. I've gained much from talking to people wiser than myself. But the moment that I sensed potential dependence creeping in, I cut ties. Similarly, it felt great to confide in my father, but I realize now that I was clinging to a comfortable blankie rather than making any real progress.

**Be willing to lose your faith** – This is a hard one. I definitely do not advocate _throwing_ your faith away. And _you_ probably can't anyway – you've got scrupulocity - haha, jokes on you. Rather, dive into what we hear from God in the Bible – the promise that if you seek, you will find. And don't think about "what if I seek in the wrong way, and run off course" – God isn't clumbsy, He knows the way He's made you and he knows the world He's made. He knows how confusing things can be. He nevertheless said that you should _seek!_ So, trust Him at His word. Dive into the mystery without having to understand what you'll find on the other side. The truth is that _your_ faith isn't correct. Remember, you serve a caricature rather than a real God. Your faith will change, you'll start serving the true God rather than your caricature of Him.

**Abandon your sanity** – Face it, we're all a little nuts. Hanging on for fear of losing your mind might actually be working against you.


### Embrace Paradox

If reason isn't working for you, then the idea here is to use it like a flamethrower to burn down all the structure around you that you've been clinging to (last section). Paradox works to address cognitive concerns because it helps you realize how out-of-sync your thoughts are with reality and rationality.

**Don't serve a petty God** – A bit of a repeat, but if God is petty, then what good does it do to obey? He's made you fallable, and you'll sooner or later fail and then just await his Cheshire grin to appear. Fortunately, He's not that. How could the originator of all the cosmos stoop to pettiness of any kind?

**You're probably evil for not just trusting in God in the first place** – Did you think about that one? While you spend your scrupulous moments worrying about trivia, you've missed the bigger picture. His relationship to you isn't about completely understanding Biblical nuance, or mental cleanliness (when you're worried about involuntary thoughts that you deem inappropriate), it's about the whole relationship with Him that you're foregoing because you're wrapped around religious bylaws rather than spiritual dependence that He seeks.

**You're wasting God's time** – Similar to the last one, I realized that God wanted me to love people, and I realized that I was spending so much time thinking about myself, "woe is me", _wishing_ things would get better, that I was literally never doing the good things God had in store for me to do. If I would only quit worrying about whether or not I was sufficiently "obedient" I would have all the time in the world and the mental and emotional space to become much more obedient. Maybe I could help someone besides myself!

**The current object of concern is _not_ the most important thing ever.** – Whatever I was preoccupied with always seemed like the most important thing ever, 10 times more important than the previous object of my concerns. But the paradox came when I realized that the ideas would cycle. I mentioned that on two occasions I had attempted to parse the liar's paradox. Both times they were the "most important thing ever", but in the interim I had traversed 10 or 20 other preoccupations that also individually seemed like the most important thing ever. Can't everything _always_ be the most important thing ever. And when you come across the same idea, then you know that the importance value is spurious. You've walked up a spiral staircase only to find yourself at the place you started.

### Change Your Behavior

This is tricky with scrupulousity. You can't well go around blaspheming, lying, and fornicating until your scrupulocity goes away. But there are things you can do.

**Delay acting out compulsions** – Did an inappropriate thought go through your head? Lean into the cognitive understanding that brains make random stuff, lean into the cognitive understanding that God actually understands this and isn't petty. And _don't_ ask forgiveness! OR Did you find a new obscure fact that might just be the key to crushing your whole faith? (This was my form of OCD.) Well lean into the fact that nothing in the past 5 years of onslaught has totally crushed it, and don't go on a 3-day odyssey of research and contemplation. Further embrace the uncertainty here: none of the other challenges have completely crushed your faith, maybe you're in denial anyway – research is pointless if you're not willing to acknowledge the consequences of what you've learned so far.

**"Waffle" on the compulsions** – This was particularly helpful for me. Rather than avoiding compulsions, do them randomly. When I felt like diving into scripture compulsively, I would mentally flip a coin and randomly decide whether to do the compulsion. If no, then I would skip that time and do it later (felt awful). If yes, then I would dive into the compulsion with at set time limit (like 5 minutes) _and_ I would require myself to focus on nothing else but the compulsion during this time. Typically – and counterintuitively – this would result in boredom.

For me "waffling" was quite affective because something else was "in charge" of my decisions to engage in the compulsion or not. I think the randomness of it all helped pull apart the stimulus-response loop of anxiety-then-compulsion, so that ultimately I didn't feel compelled to engage, and less compulsion relaxed the whole loop. It was also eye-opening that engaging was _boring!_

### "Enjoy" the Ride

I remember the day that it occurred to me that the physical aspects of my anxiety were actually quite similar to the physical experience of anticipation in a teenage romance or in riding a roller coaster. Those two things are exciting!

I was never actually able to convince myself that crippling anxiety was really me having fun. But there was a similarity, and it got me introspecting. When I was in the middle of my anxiety I was also much more interested in the "texture and the experience" of the anxiety itself. I felt the pain, but I also became interested in simultaneously watching and studying the pain from an outside perspective. Sometimes the pain wasn't so bad because it was interesting. I was my own bespectacled, white-coat scientist studying me.

This has followed me as a benefit into normal life as well. If I'm getting ready to go into a difficult discussion where I know I'm going to butt heads with someone at work, I get anxiety, and it triggers the white-coat scientist to pop out, my responses become less quick trigger and more thoughtful.

"Enjoy the ride" is also reminiscent of the earlier recommendation to just give up. If you're watching the pain as an interested 3rd party, then you're not trying to avoid it. You learn things about it. You learn when it decreases, and you might learn your own tips and tricks in time.


### Squash Your Ego

Depressed and anxious people are some of the most self-absorbed people you can ever know. I know! I've been both! It's not their fault. It's just that (in the case of anxiety) when the 5-alarm siren is blaring in your ear, it's hard to pay attention to anything else. Nevertheless, God doesn't want you to be so preoccupied with yourself. Focus on others when you feel like focusing upon yourself and you'll gain a new introspection of your own problems.

**Don't pray for yourself** – This is an odd piece of advice, but for scrupulocity I think it's a pretty good one. Prayers about "please God, solve all of _my_ problems" is often a form of compulsion, it entangles you with your ego, it _literally is helping you to practice your scrupulous anxieties!_ There's a really good chance that you're not praying to God, but you're praying to yourself or your cardboard caricature of God. _Instead,_ find a pray buddy. _Someone_ needs to pray for you. It just needs to not be you for some time. Trade – understand their concerns and practice setting aside yours for them. Know that they are going to pray for you, so that you don't have to. Don't pray for yourself!

### Hold No Talismans

If you find any little trick that "works for you" – _be wary._ If something gives you relief, then the temptation is to cling to it, and it will soon become ineffective. The whole point of all of this is in being able to learn to let go. Be happy when you find some relief, but don't demand of the universe that everything should continue to work just as you have planned. It won't and you're disappointment will be more of a setback than it really has to be.


## Epilogue

An epilogue is the little mini-chapter that you sometimes find at the end of long books... I'm sorry this post has been so long! And epilogue also tells you a bit about what happened after the end of the main story of the book.

So what of me? Am I cured? Who knows?! As part of my journey, I really did make an effort to give up on my focus to "get better", so it doesn't quite make sense to tell you an answer to that. But I can say that my particular brand of scrupulocity, and OCD, for that matter has been asymptomatic for many, many years. My marriage marks the strongest sudden change in my behavior, thought I had been working on both the cognitive and behavioral bits for years prior to that. And after that point things started ramping down over the span of 5 or so years. Some days were still filled with anguish, but there was much less time spent on trying to resolve the anguish, and probably because of that, the anguish was much decreased to the point that I don't notice it as a problem any more.

Does that mean everything it better now? Not at all! I'm a human after all. I still experience occasional periods of challenging anxiety, though they aren't nearly as challenging as my experience with scrupulocity and they aren't nearly as long-lived. I've also experienced one extended period of depression that had nothing to do with the scrupulocity which I found to be much more accutely disabling than OCD, but _fortunately_ not as long lived. Am I now innoculated to future anxiety and depression and other forms of suffering? Nope. I won't look forward to them, but if they hit, I won't make the mistake of avoiding them or pushing them away.

Does that mean that I've finally resolved all of my ethical, existential, and theological quandries? (Because, after all, that was the only way that I was going to be able to relieve my anxiety.) Goodness no! If anything, I feel that I know less now than I did before. But that's also ok. I found out that knowing all answers isn't the point of life – you've got eternity for that (and I also suspect it still won't really matter then either). Rather, God wants this life to be lived to the fullest, and being preoccupied with the answers or even with my own salvation was standing in the way of living and giving my all to this life. The important answers now are all stated simply: I believe that God _is_ and God is good. I'm not perfect, and can't be. I believe that God has made a way for me. I can't _know_ anything in the way I once wanted to – and that's ok. It's even the way things should be. I am a Christian.
